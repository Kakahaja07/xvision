Best X Bio for Startup Founders: 5 Templates by Stage

By Prashant Sharma · Oct 26, 2026 · 6 min read

What is the best X bio for startup founders?

The best X bio for startup founders names the problem the company solves, who it serves, and what the founder posts about, in under 160 characters. A strong example: "Co-founder @[Company], scheduling software for dental clinics. I post what I learn selling to small businesses." It explains both the business and the reason to follow.

Founders often treat their X bio like a company tagline. "Revolutionizing workflows with AI-powered synergy." I've read hundreds of those, and I couldn't tell you what a single one of those companies does.

Then you hit a bio like "Ex-nurse, now building an app so night-shift nurses can swap shifts in one tap." Instantly, you know the person, the product and the customer. That's the bar.

A founder's bio on X (formerly Twitter) has a harder job than most. It has to sell the person and the company at the same time, without sounding like an ad. Get it right, and every post you write sends curious people, investors and early customers to a page that makes sense.

What makes a founder bio different from a normal bio?

A founder bio is different because it serves two audiences at once: people who want to learn from you and people who might buy from you. It needs one line for the company and one line for the content you share.

X's own advice for businesses, on its getting started page, is to use the bio to "share what you do, your value, and why to follow." For founders, I'd split that into two parts:

  • The company line. What you build, and for whom.
  • The follow line. What you post that's useful even if someone never buys.

The follow line matters more than founders expect. Most people who find you won't need your product today. They'll follow for your lessons, then remember you when they do.

5 best X bio templates for startup founders

The best X bio for a startup founder depends on the company's stage. A pre-launch founder needs a waitlist. A growing company needs a clear customer. These five original templates, each with a fictional example under 125 characters by my count, cover the common stages.

1. The pre-launch builder

Template: Building [product] for [audience]. Sharing [what you share] on the way to launch. [Call to action].

"Building a simple invoicing app for freelancers. Sharing every win and mistake on the way to launch. Waitlist below." (116 characters)

This works when you have no product yet. The honesty ("win and mistake") builds trust, and the waitlist link in your website field turns interest into sign-ups.

2. The clear-customer co-founder

Template: Co-founder @[Company], [what it is] for [customer]. I post what I learn about [topic].

"Co-founder @[Company], scheduling software for dental clinics. I post what I learn selling to small businesses." (111 characters)

Naming a narrow customer, like dental clinics, is a strength, not a weakness. The right buyer feels seen, and everyone else still gets a clear content promise.

3. The domain expert

Template: Ex-[role], now building @[Company] so [audience] can [outcome]. Posts on [topic] and startups.

"Ex-nurse, now building @[Company] so night-shift nurses can swap shifts in one tap. Posts on healthcare work and startups." (122 characters)

Your past job is your proof. It tells people why you understand the problem better than an outsider.

4. The growing-company CEO

Template: CEO @[Company]. We help [customer] [result]. Writing [rhythm] about [topics].

"CEO @[Company]. We help online stores cut return costs. Writing weekly about pricing, hiring and founder mistakes." (114 characters)

Once the company is known, the bio can lean on the brand. The topics list tells people what kind of founder content to expect.

5. The solo or indie founder

Template: Solo founder. [Short, human detail]. I share [numbers or lessons] [rhythm].

"Solo founder. Two products, one coffee budget. I share monthly revenue, what worked and what flopped." (101 characters)

Personality carries this one. It fits founders who build in public on Twitter, where sharing real numbers is the content.

Should founders mention their company in the bio?

Yes, most founders should mention their company in the bio, usually as its @handle. It connects your personal account to the brand account and helps people check that you are who you say you are.

Keep the company mention short, though. One handle and one plain description is enough. Your personal account grows best when it feels like a person, not a second brand page.

If your company account is a business, X lets it convert to a free Professional Account. X's Professional Accounts help page says this adds a Professional Category shown on the profile, and it requires a complete profile with an account name, a bio and a profile picture.

What should founders leave out of their X bio?

Founders should leave out buzzwords, long lists of titles, and claims they can't back up. Each one uses space that could explain what you actually do.

  • Jargon. "AI-native platform for synergy" says nothing. "Software that reads invoices for you" says everything.
  • Title stacks. "Founder | Investor | Advisor | Speaker" splits attention four ways.
  • Vanity claims. Only list revenue, funding or awards if they're true and still current.
  • Every past company. Pick the one that best proves you understand this problem.

How does the bio fit into a founder's wider profile?

The bio is the promise, and the rest of the profile is the proof. A founder's header can show the product, and the pinned post can tell the company story or share your best lesson.

I'd pin one of two things: a short thread on why you started the company, or your most useful post for your target customer. The guide on creating a good Twitter pinned post covers the options. For the writing itself, the best Twitter bio examples for creators show more structures you can borrow.

Then put the profile to work. The guides on growing a founder account on X and getting followers on Twitter as a founder cover what to post once the profile is ready.

Write the bio your first customer would understand

Here's a simple test. Imagine your ideal first customer reads your bio for three seconds. Would they know what you build and whether it's for them? If yes, you're done. If not, cut words until they would.

You can change your bio any time, and it costs nothing to try a new version. Pick the template that matches your stage, fill it with honest details, and let your posts do the rest. A clear founder bio is a small thing that keeps paying off with every post you write.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a startup founder use a personal or company account on X?

Most startup founders do best with both: a personal account for lessons and opinions, and a company account for product news. Each account then has one clear job for its followers. X's authenticity policy allows up to 10 accounts with different, non-duplicative purposes, so running two accounts is within the rules.

Should founders put funding or revenue in their X bio?

Founders can put funding or revenue in their X bio if the numbers are true, current and help the reader trust them. A line like "backed by [fund]" can help with hiring. Leave numbers out if they are outdated, because a stale claim does more harm than having no claim.

How long should a founder's X bio be?

A founder's X bio can be up to 160 characters, which is X's limit for every bio. Around 100 to 125 characters usually fits a company line and a content line. Shorter bios read faster, so trim adjectives first and keep the customer and the problem.

Can founders use emojis in their X bio?

Yes, founders can use emojis in their X bio, but one or two is enough. A single emoji can mark a location or a product category. A bio packed with emojis looks less serious to investors and business buyers, so most founders keep the tone clean and plain.

Where should a founder put the company website on X?

A founder should put the company or waitlist link in the website field of the X profile, which X lists separately from the 160-character bio. That keeps the bio free for describing the company and content. Use one link that matches the main call to action in the bio.
