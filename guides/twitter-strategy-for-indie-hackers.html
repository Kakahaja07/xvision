Twitter Strategy for Indie Hackers: Grow on X and Still Ship

By Prashant Sharma · Oct 26, 2026 · 6 min read

What is the best Twitter strategy for indie hackers?

The best Twitter strategy for indie hackers is to cap X at about 30 to 45 minutes a day, aim posts at the customers of your product rather than only at other makers, share progress weekly and turn every shipped feature into a small launch. Treat X as distribution time, not a second job.

Imagine an indie hacker who spends six months posting about code, coffee and productivity. The account reaches 2,000 followers. Then the product launches, and almost nobody buys. A quick look at the followers explains it: they're nearly all other makers. Kind people, great feedback, but not customers.

That trap is easy to fall into on X (formerly Twitter), and it's just as easy to avoid once you see it. Here's the plan I'd follow as a solo builder with limited hours.

What makes a Twitter strategy for indie hackers different?

A Twitter strategy for indie hackers is different because one person does everything: building, support, marketing and sales. Every minute on X comes straight out of build time, so the plan has to be small, repeatable and tied to revenue.

The best-known solo makers show it can work. Pieter Levels writes on his blog that he builds startups like Nomads.com, Remote OK and Photo AI by himself, all bootstrapped. You don't need to copy his style. The lesson is that a solo builder can use public posting as the main distribution channel, as long as it's consistent.

Should indie hackers talk to makers or customers on X?

Indie hackers should talk to both, but they should aim most posts at customers. Makers give feedback, support and early users for dev tools. Customers pay. If your product isn't for makers, a maker-only audience won't pay the bills.

Maker audienceCustomer audience
Gives youFeedback, motivation, launch supportRevenue, real use cases, referrals
Where to find themBuild-in-public posts, maker CommunitiesNiche conversations about their problem
What they want to readNumbers, stack choices, lessonsHow to solve their problem faster

A simple split works: about two posts in three for your customers, one in three for fellow makers. If you sell to developers or other founders, the two audiences overlap and you're in luck.

How much time should an indie hacker spend on X?

An indie hacker should spend about 30 to 45 minutes a day on X, in one or two fixed blocks. A time box protects build time and stops the endless scroll that feels like marketing but isn't.

  • 15 minutes, morning: reply to 5 to 10 posts from your customers' world.
  • 10 minutes: answer every reply and DM from yesterday.
  • 10 to 20 minutes, after a work session: write one post from what you just did or learned.

The reply habit matters most while your account is small. The engagement strategy for small accounts explains how to write replies that get noticed. Close the tab when the timer ends. This part is actually really easy once it's a habit, and the product keeps moving.

What should indie hackers post on X?

Indie hackers should post three kinds of things: posts that help customers with their problem, short progress updates on the product, and small launch posts when something ships. Each one has a different job.

Customer posts grow the right audience. Progress posts build trust and keep makers cheering. Launch posts convert. If you want the full playbook for progress posts, my guide on how to build in public on Twitter covers what to share and how often. For finding customer conversations to join, use the search steps in how to find customers on Twitter.

How do Communities and Lists help indie hackers?

Communities and Lists help indie hackers by cutting the noise. A Community puts you in a room of people who share an interest. A private List lets you check your best customers and peers in minutes instead of scrolling the whole feed.

According to X's help page on Communities, posts you share in a Community can also show up in search and on your profile. Join one maker Community and one Community where your customers gather. Read the rules, help first and share your product only where it's welcome.

How do you turn shipping into distribution?

You turn shipping into distribution by treating every meaningful release as a mini launch: a short demo, the problem it solves and a thank-you to whoever asked for it. Building and marketing become the same activity.

Free tools are a classic indie move too. A small, useful tool related to your product gives people a reason to share a link that isn't an ad. For big releases, follow the full checklist in my guide on how to launch a product on X.

What should indie hackers avoid on X?

Indie hackers should avoid engagement groups, follow-for-follow schemes and copy-paste replies. They inflate numbers without bringing customers, and X's rules ban several of them outright.

X's authenticity policy lists coordinating to exchange engagement (likes, replies, reposts or follows) and "follow churn" as not allowed. Beyond the rules, those numbers lie to you. You'll think a post worked when it only worked inside the group.

What should indie hackers measure?

Indie hackers should measure signups or sales per post first, then who is engaging with them. Follower count matters less than whether the people replying could ever buy.

Add a tracking tag to every product link you post. Then check who actually interacts with you. If it's all makers, shift more posts toward customer problems. For turning a small audience into income, see how to monetize a small Twitter account.

Small daily effort, compounding results

A Twitter strategy for indie hackers works when it fits around building, not on top of it. Time-box X, aim most posts at customers, share progress, launch every release and measure sales, not applause. Do that for a few months and X becomes the distribution channel you never had to pay for. You've got this.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should indie hackers create a separate X account for each product?

Most indie hackers should post from one personal account rather than separate accounts for each product. A personal account carries trust from one product to the next. A product account can still help for support and release notes, but splitting attention across many accounts slows growth for a solo builder on X.

How many followers does an indie hacker need to make sales on X?

An indie hacker does not need a set number of followers to make sales on X. What matters is how many followers have the problem the product solves. A few hundred well-matched followers can bring early sales, while thousands of fellow makers may bring none for a non-developer product.

Are engagement groups allowed on X?

No, coordinated engagement groups break X's rules. X's authenticity policy does not allow coordinating to exchange likes, replies, reposts or follows, or paying others to inflate account metrics. Beyond the risk to the account, these groups give indie hackers false signals about which posts actually reach customers.

Is X still worth it for indie hackers in 2026?

Yes, X is still worth it for many indie hackers in 2026, especially those selling to founders, developers, marketers and creators. It is free, public and fast. Indie hackers selling to audiences that rarely use X should test it for about 90 days before deciding to keep it.

What is the difference between indie hacking and building in public?

Indie hacking means building a profitable business on your own or with a tiny team, usually without outside funding. Building in public is a way of sharing that work openly on platforms like X. Many indie hackers build in public, but it is optional, not a requirement for success.
