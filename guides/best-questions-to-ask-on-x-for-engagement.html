The Best Questions to Ask on X for Engagement (40 Examples)

By Prashant Sharma · Oct 26, 2026 · 6 min read

What are the best questions to ask on X for engagement?

The best questions to ask on X for engagement are specific, personal and quick to answer, such as "What's the one tool you'd keep if you lost the rest?" They work because anyone can answer from experience in one line. As of October 2026, X's open-source ranking code weights a reply at 5.0 compared with 0.66 for a like.

Picture two posts on the same topic. One asks "Thoughts on productivity?" The other asks "What's the first thing you do when you sit down to work?" The first one asks you to write an essay. The second one you can answer before you finish reading it. Based on what I've seen, that difference decides whether a question gets replies.

A question post is any post that invites readers to share an answer. On X (formerly Twitter), good questions matter because replies outweigh likes in X's ranking code, and because a question is the simplest way to start a conversation with people who don't know you yet.

What makes a question good for engagement?

A good engagement question is easy, personal and specific. Readers should know their answer within three seconds, and no answer should feel wrong.

Here's a quick test. Before you post a question, check it against these four points:

  • Answerable in one line. Nobody wants to write an essay in the replies.
  • Based on experience, not knowledge. "What's your favorite..." beats "What is the correct..." because nobody can get it wrong.
  • Specific. "What's your morning routine?" is vague. "What's the first app you open in the morning?" is easy.
  • Relevant to your niche. The answers should teach you something about your audience. If you're not sure who that is yet, start with finding your target audience on Twitter.

The weights behind this come from X's current ranking weights, where, as of October 2026, a reply scores 5.0 and a like 0.66. X updates that file often, so treat the numbers as a snapshot. The older 2023 release showed the same pattern, with 13.5 for a reply and 0.5 for a like.

40 questions to ask on X, by type

These 40 questions are grouped by the kind of reply they invite. Change the topic to fit your niche, keep the shape, and they'll work for most audiences.

"One thing" questions (easy and fast)

  1. What's the one tool you'd keep if you lost all the others?
  2. What's one habit that changed your work more than you expected?
  3. What's one book you recommend more than any other?
  4. What's one setting everyone should change on their phone?
  5. What's one thing you stopped doing this year and don't miss?

Experience questions (stories in the replies)

  1. What's the best advice you ignored for too long?
  2. What was your first job, and what did it teach you?
  3. What's a mistake you made early that you'd warn others about?
  4. When did you realize you were good at what you do now?
  5. What's the worst meeting you've ever sat through?

Opinion questions (gentle debate)

  1. Is it better to start with a small niche or a broad topic?
  2. What's an "unpopular" opinion you have about your industry?
  3. Which skill is overrated right now?
  4. Which skill is underrated right now?
  5. What advice in your field do you think is wrong?

Help-me-decide questions (great for building in public)

  1. Which of these two headlines would you click? (Show both.)
  2. Should I launch this week or polish for another month?
  3. Which feature should I build next: A or B?
  4. Does this landing page explain what the product does in five seconds?
  5. What price would you expect for this?

These work especially well if you build in public on X, because people love helping with a real decision.

"Teach me" questions (you learn too)

  1. What's a beginner question in your field that's actually hard to answer?
  2. What's the simplest way to explain what you do to a ten-year-old?
  3. What's a free resource that taught you more than a paid course?
  4. What's one shortcut you use every day?
  5. What would you learn first if you started over today?

Fill-in-the-gap questions (low effort, high fun)

  1. Finish this sentence: "The best part of working from home is..."
  2. Describe your job badly in five words.
  3. Your work week in one emoji?
  4. Name a tool you use that nobody else seems to know about.
  5. Explain your niche using only a movie title.

Recommendation questions (useful threads people save)

  1. What's the best podcast episode you heard this month?
  2. Who is one small account in your niche more people should follow?
  3. What's the best small purchase you've made for your work this year?
  4. What's a newsletter you actually open every week?
  5. What's a YouTube channel that taught you a real skill?

Reflection questions (deeper replies)

  1. What's something you believed a year ago that you don't now?
  2. What are you working on that nobody knows about yet?
  3. What does a good day at work look like for you?
  4. What's one goal you're quietly working toward this month?
  5. What would you tell yourself on your first day in this field?

Should you use polls or open questions?

Use polls when you want quick votes and open questions when you want replies and stories. Polls are easier to answer, but they mostly collect votes, not conversation.

According to X's help page About X Polls, a poll can have up to four choices of 25 characters each and can run from 5 minutes to 7 days, with 1 day as the default. Votes are private, so neither you nor anyone else sees who picked what.

My favorite move is to combine the two. Post a poll, then add "Tell me why in the replies." The poll gets the easy votes, and the follow-up gets the stories.

Which questions should you avoid?

Avoid bait questions that ask for empty actions, such as "Who else is grinding today?" or "Drop your link below." They get quick replies but rarely start real conversations.

Questions like these are so common that XVision, the free extension I built, treats them as engagement bait and collapses them in the feed by default. Based on what I've seen, many readers scroll past them anyway. Ask what you actually want to know. Real curiosity shows, and people respond to it.

Ask, then show up

The best questions to ask on X for engagement are only half the job. The other half is replying to the answers. Answer early, ask follow-ups, and thank people for good ideas.

Pick three questions from the list above that fit your niche and try one a week, mixed in with your usual posts. If you need ideas for the other days, see what to post on X every day. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I ask questions on X?

Asking one or two questions a week on X is a good starting point for most accounts. Questions in every post can start to feel forced. Mix them with tips, opinions and stories, and save questions for topics where you genuinely want to read the answers and reply to them.

Why does nobody answer my questions on X?

Questions on X often go unanswered because they are too broad, too hard, or posted when few followers are online. "Thoughts?" asks a lot of the reader. A specific question like "What's the first app you open each morning?" is easy to answer and gets more replies.

Do questions work for small accounts?

Yes, questions work for small accounts on X, but expect a few replies at first, not dozens. A small audience means fewer people see the question. Replying to others in your niche every day grows the pool of people who know you and will answer when you ask.

Should I use hashtags on question posts?

Hashtags are optional on question posts. X's help center recommends no more than 2 hashtags per post as best practice. A relevant hashtag can help people find a question through search, but a clear, specific question matters far more than any tag for getting replies.

What is an example of a good engagement question?

A good engagement question on X is short, personal and easy to answer, like "What's one tool you'd keep if you lost all the others?" Anyone in a niche can answer it in one line, no answer is wrong, and the replies teach you what your audience actually uses.
